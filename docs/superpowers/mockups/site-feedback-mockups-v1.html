Toolkit · Testing a claim (2b)

What makes a strong “Why”

Use these six checks to justify each method in your 2(b) matrix. Justify; don’t critique.

01

Reputable organisation

Is the information from an organisation with research expertise and a reputation to protect?

For the vehicle-crime claimNational police statistics office, not a car-alarm seller’s blog.

Links to: Reliability · Q3

Which is stronger? An insurer’s published claims data, or one garage owner? The insurer: national records, and data it has to account for.

02

Traceable data

Does the source say where its numbers come from, so someone else could check them?

For the vehicle-crime claimA report that names its survey, dates and sample size.

Links to: Accuracy of facts

True or false? “A statistic with no source is fine if it looks official.” False: if it can’t be checked, it can’t be trusted.

03

Expertise or experience

Does the person or group know the topic from training, or from living through it?

For the vehicle-crime claimA traffic police officer, not a manager who joined last month.

Links to: Who column

Name one expert you could interview for this claim, and why. E.g. a police crime analyst, who sees theft reports every week.

04

Covers the whole claim

Does the evidence reach as far as the claim does: national? “many”? over time?

For the vehicle-crime claim“Across the country” needs national figures, not one firm.

Links to: Split the claim

Which word sets the scope? “Many schools recycle waste food.” “Many”: you need a count across lots of schools.

05

Comparable numbers

Can the figures be compared fairly between groups or years (same measure, same definition)?

For the vehicle-crime claimThefts per year, 2015–2025, counted the same way each year.

Links to: “going up”

What’s missing? “Thefts in 2025: 4,000.” A comparison. You need earlier years to show “going up”.

06

Triangulation

Will you compare two or more methods, and say what it means if they agree?

For the vehicle-crime claimPolice figures + insurance claims + a driver survey all rising → claim supported.

Links to: compare line

Finish the line: “If both methods show a rise…” “…the claim is more likely to be true, because two independent sources agree.”

The student asked whether anything had been taken from the company vans. Mr Grant said one van had been broken into the week before and some tools had gone…

Because of this one recent theft, the student decided that vehicle crime is probably rising across the whole country.

What Q2(a) really asks

You are judging the method, not the topic: who was asked, how, where, how it was recorded, and whether the conclusion fits. Every point is a three-step chain.

First, name what the researcher did. Quote it or name it from Source 3. Then explain the effect on the evidence: does it make the data more or less accurate, honest, complete or representative? Finally, link back to what the research was trying to find out.

Some candidates focused only on strengths or only on weaknesses. Always cover both. Level 4 names both strengths and weaknesses.
